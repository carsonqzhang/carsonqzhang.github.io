About

I am a PhD Candidate in Economics at the University of California, Los Angeles and an affiliate of the California Center for Population Research (CCPR), where I expect to graduate in 2028.

My work focuses on how health care access, early-life conditions, and genetics shape health over the life course. I combine large-scale linked administrative and survey data with quasi-experimental and structural methods to study the long-run returns to universal health coverage, the historical emergence of income gradients in longevity, and the gene-environment interaction in health.

Working Papers

The Long Run Effects of Free Childhood Health Care Access: Evidence from the National Health Service in the UK
▸ Abstract ▸ Presentations
This paper estimates the long-run health effects of universal childhood health care access in the UK. The design compares cohorts born in different years relative to the 1948 introduction of the National Health Service (NHS) across birthplaces with different pre-reform health care resources. The NHS immediately leads to lower infant mortality and higher birth weights. Using linked administrative records of approximately 320,000 individuals, I find that free childhood access reduces disease incidence across multiple systems in later life (ages 60–75), and increases educational attainment and the probability of staying employed. Despite modest individual effects, universal coverage yields fiscal savings that exceed costs and large health gains for the initially affected cohorts.

The Association Between Income and Adult Life Expectancy in the United States Emerged in the Mid 20th Century
with Andrew Goodman-Bacon, Adriana Lleras-Muney, Joseph Price, and Dahai Yue
▸ Abstract ▸ Presentations
Today, at age 40, the rich can expect to live almost a decade longer than the poor. However, we find no differences in adult period life expectancy by income in 1940 or 1950. We also find no positive association between income proxies and adult life expectancy for men or women between 1900 and 1950. Two factors help explain this: the rich lived in cities where life expectancy was lower and were more likely to consume goods that cause chronic diseases. Thus income-related disparities in period life expectancy likely emerged around the 1950s, a period of low income inequality but rapid medical innovation. As a result, cohorts who lived more of their adult lives after 1950 had steeper income gradients in realized lifespans.
Presentations: 3rd All-UC Demography Conference
When Beliefs and Reality Clash: The Impact of Survival Belief Biases on Saving and Wealth Distribution
with Yaohui Zhao
▸ Abstract ▸ Presentations
This paper studies how biases in survival beliefs, defined as the difference between objective and subjective life expectancy (LE), affect savings decisions and wealth distribution in China, where subjective LE is self-reported and objective LE is model-predicted. We first document that younger individuals (aged 45–70) underestimate their survival chances, while older individuals (70–95) overestimate them. These biases appear to be driven by insufficient adjustments of survival beliefs to new health shocks. Using a standard life-cycle model, we show that the age pattern would cause the younger group to undersave by 8.1% (942 RMB), and the older group to oversave by 13.0% (1,226 RMB). More importantly, undersaving at young leads to 36.5% (28,160 RMB) less wealth by age 70, which cannot be compensated by oversaving during the old. Additionally, we find that rural residents, who have a shorter objective LE than urban residents, also tend to underestimate their future survival, thus having a much lower subjective LE. This accounts for 8.4% of the urban-rural wealth disparities, with a significant portion driven by the underestimation of survival among rural residents. Our empirical analysis supports the model prediction: those who overestimate their survival chances tend to save more. Overall, our findings support a new health-wealth channel, namely time preference caused by survival belief differences.
